1 Introduction
Human perception uses linguistic or visual cues to identify targets, attend to visual and auditory evidence
across space and time, and form goal-directed judgments. Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) jointly
process images, videos, audio, and text, supporting multimodal understanding and reasoning (Bai et al., 2025;
Xu et al., 2025b; Google, 2025). Recent MLLMs can revisit relevant temporal clips (Yang et al., 2026a; Zeng
et al., 2026b), inspect task-relevant spatial evidence (Wang et al., 2026c; Meng et al., 2026), and selectively
acquire audio-visual evidence during reasoning (Xing et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026). These advances motivate
evaluating omni-modal video grounding and understanding. This requires identifying targets from textual or
visual cues, grounding them spatiotemporally, and integrating audio-visual evidence to understand events.
With the rapid development of MLLMs, how to comprehensively evaluate their performance in complex
video understanding tasks has become an important problem. Table 1 compares existing benchmarks across
four dimensions. (i) Target Specification defines how targets are presented: through only textual queries (Fu
et al., 2025a) or with reference images (Ma et al., 2026). (ii) Video Evidence distinguishes tasks resolved
from video frames (Chen et al., 2025a) from those requiring synchronized audio-visual content (Hong et al.,
2026). (iii) Task Coverage specifies the output: temporal grounding (TG) and spatial grounding (SG) predict
target-specific intervals and regions (Lei et al., 2021; Cheng et al., 2026), while question answering (QA)
predicts answers (Fu et al., 2026). (iv) Video Duration determines the temporal scope: short-video benchmarks
emphasize fine-grained motion and local dynamics (Li et al., 2024b), whereas long-video benchmarks test
long-range dependencies and information integration across temporally distant events (Zhou et al., 2025a).
Despite this progress, existing benchmarks have three limitations in systematically diagnosing omni-modal
video grounding and understanding. (i) Limited video-evidence coverage. Benchmarks typically assess
either visual information (Chen et al., 2025a) or synchronized audio-visual content (Hong et al., 2026). In
news broadcasts, some queries concern visible scenes; others require aligning spoken reports with people
or events. Both settings are needed to assess visual perception and audio-visual integration. (ii) Inflexible
target-specification settings. Most benchmarks use only text (Fu et al., 2026), while IV-Bench combines

To address these gaps, we introduce OmniCompass, a holistic benchmark for omni-modal video grounding
and understanding. As shown in Figure 2, OmniCompass has four key features: (i) Complementary
Video Evidence. OmniCompass-VL evaluates understanding from visual content, while OmniCompass-AV
additionally requires audio. (ii) Flexible Target Specification. Each instance pairs Text and Text+Image
settings, specifying the same target through language alone or with a reference image. The video, task semantics,
and ground truth are the same. We introduce Cross-Specification Consistency Scores (CS) to jointly measure
task accuracy and prediction consistency across these settings. (iii) Unified Task Coverage. Both tracks
provide TG, SG, and QA tasks per video. The VL track covers five perception and three reasoning categories,
whereas the AV track covers three perception and two reasoning categories. (iv) Diverse Durations and
Content. OmniCompass-VL contains 820 videos and 2,460 instances across short, medium, and long durations.
It combines public sports, traffic, surveillance, and documentary videos with 240 self-collected movie, TV,
variety, and news videos. This subset forms OmniCompass-AV with 720 instances.
We evaluate 50 MLLMs, including proprietary and open-source models. The best proprietary model
exceeds the best open-source model by 3.2% in CSAvg on VL track and 32.8% on AV track. Strong QA
performance coexists with weak grounding and cross-reference inconsistency, even when aggregate accuracies
are similar across settings. Visual target specifications do not consistently help: Text+Image improves the
single-setting average for only 11 of 36 models. Further analyses show that timestamps substantially improve
model performance across tasks and tracks, while audio consistently improves QA and TG performance on AV
track. Future MLLMs therefore need stronger cross-modal alignment and more reliable audio-aware grounding.
Our contributions are as follows:
1. A Holistic Evaluation Framework. OmniCompass unifies target-guided omni-modal video grounding
and understanding across four dimensions: paired Text and Text+Image specifications, VL/AV evidence
tracks, TG/SG/QA tasks, and short-to-long videos. We introduce CS to jointly assess task correctness and
consistency across paired target specifications.
2. A Diverse, Rigorously Verified Benchmark. OmniCompass spans 820 videos, 80.6 hours, seven domains,

2 Related Work
2.1 Multimodal Large Language Models for Video Understanding
MLLMs connect language models with multimodal encoders for perception and reasoning (Liu et al., 2024a;
Chen et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2025a; Google, 2025; Lin et al., 2025a,b; An et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026b).
LLaVA-OneVision (Li et al., 2024a) and LLaVA-Video (Zhang et al., 2024) unify image-video modeling. The
latter uses synthetic captions and question-answering data for video instruction tuning. LLaVA-OneVision-2 (An
et al., 2026) further adopts OneVision-Encoder (Tang et al., 2026) for native-resolution visual encoding and
efficient long-video processing through codec-aware token compression. VideoLLaMA3 (Zhang et al., 2025)
and InternVL3.5 (Wang et al., 2025b) strengthen video training and reasoning. Qwen3-VL (Bai et al., 2025)
improves long-context video understanding through temporal alignment, while Qwen3-Omni (Xu et al., 2025b)
jointly processes vision, audio, and language. Keye-VL-2.0 (Team et al., 2026) adapts DSA for efficient longcontext processing and consolidates task-specific capabilities through cross-modal MOPD. TimeLens (Zhang
et al., 2026) improves temporal grounding with interleaved timestamps and thinking-free reinforcement learning,
and TimeLens2 (Zhu et al., 2026) further broadens task coverage through verified multi-interval supervision
and temporal Wasserstein rewards. Agentic extensions also explore tool-assisted video reasoning to selectively
acquire task-relevant visual and audio evidence (Wang et al., 2026a; Yang et al., 2026a,b; Meng et al., 2026;
Xing et al., 2026). In brief, modern omni-modal video understanding identifies referenced targets and grounds
evidence in time and space by integrating visual and audio information.

2.2 Video Understanding Benchmarks
Table 1 compares existing benchmarks across four design dimensions. (i) Target Specification defines how the
target is presented. Most benchmarks use textual questions or instructions (Fu et al., 2025a; Cheng et al., 2026),
while IV-Bench (Ma et al., 2026) pairs text with a reference image for visual specification. (ii) Video Evidence
distinguishes visual and audio-visual requirements. Vision-language benchmarks assess appearance, motion, and
temporal relations. TempCompass (Liu et al., 2024b) evaluates temporal perception, while MotionBench (Hong
et al., 2025) targets fine-grained motion. WorldSense (Hong et al., 2026) and JointAVBench (Chao et al.,
2026) require synchronized visual and native-audio evidence. (iii) Task Coverage defines the expected output.
Question-answering benchmarks (Fu et al., 2025a; Li et al., 2026) produce semantic responses, while grounding
benchmarks require explicit evidence localization. OmniGround evaluates temporal intervals and spatial
regions (Gao et al., 2026), and V-STaR (Cheng et al., 2026) jointly assesses TG, SG, and QA. (iv) Video
Duration sets the temporal scope for evidence integration. MVBench emphasizes local actions and temporal
dynamics (Li et al., 2024b), whereas LongVideoBench (Wu et al., 2024), MLVU (Zhou et al., 2025a), and
LVBench (Wang et al., 2025a) stress long-context management across distant moments. LongVideoBench also
evaluates referring reasoning over interleaved video frames and subtitles. Existing benchmarks cover selected
dimensions or fixed combinations, limiting diagnosis of target identification, grounding, and omni-modal
reasoning across different target and evidence settings.
OmniCompass unifies these dimensions through paired target-specification settings, complementary
evidence tracks, and TG, SG, and QA across video durations. It supports diagnosis of target identification,
spatio-temporal grounding, and event understanding under paired reference settings.

3.2 Benchmark Construction
Constructing OmniCompass requires approximately 1,800 human-hours for paired annotation, targeted revision,
and multi-round quality verification, following the pipeline in Figure 3.
Stage 1: Video Collection and Curation. We construct a pool of 8,489 candidate videos from nine public
datasets and supplement it with 240 self-collected videos from films, TV series, variety shows, and news
programs. The resulting pool covers seven high-level domains. Appendix Table C1 details the selection scope,
characteristics, usage terms, and contributions of the public datasets to the final benchmark.
We use Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct to profile each public candidate with structured entity, action,
attribute, and scene cues. Excluding videos shorter than 30 seconds or missing any of the four referential
cue types leaves 6,946 eligible videos. We rank them by four predefined criteria: reference necessity, grounding
suitability, temporal-spatial utility, and evidence diversity (Appendix Table C2). Shortlisting follows domain,
duration, source, and subcategory constraints. Manual review retains 580 high-quality videos; with 240
self-collected videos, OmniCompass-VL contains 820 videos. The same 240 videos form OmniCompass-AV,
with additional audio-visual task annotations. Appendix C details profiling, filtering, and scoring.
Stage 2: Task Annotation and Construction. A lead expert provides two days of task-specific training to four
professional annotators before full-scale annotation. The team follows four principles to ensure consistent target
specification and evidence across TG, SG, and QA under paired Text and Text+Image settings.
(i) Same-Target Pairing. Each Text and Text+Image pair shares the same video, target, task semantics,
and ground truth. In Text+Image, the textual expression requires the reference image to identify the target.
(ii) Diverse Reference Types and Sources. References cover entity, action, attribute, and scene cues

independently construct paired Text and Text+Image TG, SG, and QA instances. AV cases include native audio. Stage 3:

the annotated event or answer evidence. For video-internal references, annotators favor a separate view outside

and three duration types. Each video receives one TG, one SG, and one QA instance under paired Text and
Text+Image settings, yielding 180 task instances. The pilot exposes inconsistencies in target pairing, temporal
boundaries, spatial target consistency, and QA evidence. The lead expert reviews these cases with the team,
resolves ambiguities, and refines the guidelines. The annotators then independently construct the remaining
instances under the calibrated protocol. Guideline training, pilot calibration, and initial task construction across
both tracks and all three tasks account for approximately 960 annotator-hours.
Stage 3: Quality Verification and Finalization. All annotated cases undergo case-level quality verification
before release. Four annotators independently cross-check annotations from their peers against the Stage 2
principles: same-target pairing, reference diversity and non-leakage, precise TG/SG labels, and evidencegrounded QA design. They inspect temporal-event completeness, boundary-frame box alignment, and the
uniqueness and evidential support of QA answers. For AV cases, they also verify native-audio dependence
and audio-visual alignment. They correct clear annotation errors, recheck the revised cases, and submit
the confirmed cases to the lead expert. The expert resolves remaining inconsistencies, makes necessary
corrections, performs final acceptance, and audits the release-ready data. These steps require approximately
320 annotator-hours and 500 lead-expert-hours. Appendix D details the process. Tables D1 and D2 report
video-level verification results and typical issues; Table D3 details human effort by stage and role.

4.1 Experimental Setting
Evaluated Models. We evaluate 50 models across tracks. OmniCompass-VL evaluates vision-language
models including proprietary models Gemini series (Google, 2025), GPT series (OpenAI, 2026a,b,c), Seed
series (Seed, 2026), and the Qwen close-source series (Qwen Team, 2026a). The open-source models cover
Kimi-K3 (Kimi Team et al., 2026), GLM-5.3-Flash (Zeng et al., 2026a), DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash (DeepSeek-AI,
2026), MiMo-V2.5 (MiMo-V2.5), the Qwen open-source series (Qwen Team, 2026b; Team, 2026a; Bai
et al., 2025), InternVL3.5-38B (Wang et al., 2025b), Keye-VL-2.0-30B-A3B (Team et al., 2026), TimeLens
series (Zhang et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2026), and the LLaVA series (An et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2024).
OmniCompass-AV evaluates omni-modal models with native audio support, including the Gemini series,
Qwen-Omni series (Team, 2026b; Qwen Team, Alibaba, 2025), and Seed-2.0-Lite/Mini (Seed, 2026), as well
as MiMo-V2.5 (MiMo-V2.5), video-SALMONN 2+ (Tang et al., 2025), MiniCPM-o-4.5-8B (Cui et al., 2026),
Ola-7B (Liu et al., 2025), VideoLLaMA2-7B (Cheng et al., 2024), and VITA-1.5-7B (Fu et al., 2025b).
Implementation Details. (i) Frame sampling: We uniformly sample 64 frames for proprietary models and
open-source models with over 100B total parameters, and 32 for smaller models. GPT-series models use 49
frames to accommodate the Text+Image reference within the 50-image limit. VITA-1.5-7B follows its official
16-frame limit. Following TimeLens (Zhang et al., 2026), each frame is immediately preceded by its timestamp
in seconds. (ii) Input modalities: OmniCompass-VL excludes audio, while OmniCompass-AV adds the
native audio following WorldSense (Hong et al., 2026) and Video-MME-v2 (Fu et al., 2026). In Text+Image,
the reference image follows these inputs and immediately precedes the task prompt. Appendix E details the
experimental setup, input serialization, and task-specific prompts and response formats.

Impact of Input Modalities. Figure 7 compares task-level CS across Text-only, Vision, and Vision+Audio
for four OmniCompass-AV models. All comparisons use matched eligible pairs within each model and task.
Text-only retains both question wordings but removes all media, including reference images. Its CSTG is only
0.1–0.8%, versus 18.8%–30.1% in CSQA . Vision improves all 12 model-task comparisons. Adding audio
raises CSQA by 18.6%–28.1%, averaging 22.9%. Gemini-3.8-Flash and Qwen3.8-Omni-Flash gain 29.4%
and 11.1% in CSTG , respectively, whereas MiMo-V2.5 loses 1.5%. CSSG changes range from -0.4% to +1.8%.
Thus, adding audio consistently improves CSQA across all four tested models, whereas TG gains vary by model
and SG gains remain limited. Additional analyses are provided in Appendix B.

5 Conclusion
In this paper, we introduce OmniCompass, a holistic benchmark for omni-modal video grounding and
understanding. It unifies TG, SG, and QA across VL and AV tracks with paired Text and Text+Image
specifications for the same video, target, task semantics, and ground truth. The benchmark covers 820 manually
annotated videos across seven domains, including a 240-video AV subset with native audio. The paired settings
and CS metric assess both prediction correctness and consistency under changes in target specification. Leading
proprietary models outperform open-source models, especially in audio-visual understanding, while precise
grounding, fine-grained perception, and audio-visual event binding remain challenging. Reference images yield
task-dependent effects, while timestamps improve model performance in both tracks. These findings motivate
stronger multimodal alignment and audio-aware grounding to connect target specifications with the relevant
events and evidence. OmniCompass supports joint evaluation of QA, precise grounding, and cross-reference
consistency across tasks and modalities, providing a basis for diagnosing model limitations and measuring
progress toward reliable omni-modal video understanding.

B Additional Analyses
B.1 Impact of Frame Scaling
Increasing the frame budget improves performance in most settings, but the effect depends on the track and task.
On OmniCompass-VL, all three tasks improve significantly for both models from 8 to 128 frames. TG CS
increases by 22.2% and 19.6% for Gemini-3.8-Flash and Qwen3.5-397B-A17B, respectively; SG increases by
22.8% and 16.9%, while QA increases by 20.0% and 18.6%. The gains are smaller and more task-dependent
on OmniCompass-AV. SG improves by 18.3% for Gemini-3.8-Flash and 22.3% for Qwen3.8-Omni-Flash,
while QA improves by 9.9% and 13.4% from 8 to 128 frames. In contrast, TG remains nearly unchanged.
We suppose that audio already provides useful temporal cues for AV TG (see Figure 7), leaving denser visual
sampling with limited additional temporal evidence, whereas SG and QA can benefit from finer spatial details
and richer visual context. Besides, more frames also increase visual input and inference cost, so the preferred
budget depends on the task and track rather than always favoring the largest setting.

B.3 Cross-Specification Consistency
Figure B3 compares paired CS with setting-specific performance. Across the ten models, TG and SG CS
average 5.0% and 5.7% below the lower single-setting mean. This gap includes both the cost of requiring
overlap with ground truth under both settings and the additional prediction-to-prediction overlap constraint.
The latter contributes only 0.3%–1.8% across the model–task combinations. The full gap does not measure
prediction disagreement alone.
For QA, 10.5–25.1% of eligible pairs are answered correctly under exactly one setting. Kimi-K3 reaches
nearly identical accuracies of 73.0% and 72.9%, yet answers only 67.6% correctly under both. Its 808 pairs
comprise 546 correct under both settings, 44 only under Text, 43 only under Text+Image, and 175 under neither.
Similar aggregate accuracy can therefore mask different instance-level outcomes. Neither-correct pairs may
still contain two different wrong answers.

We use Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct (Bai et al., 2025) to profile 8,489 domain-aligned public candidates. The
outputs describe entities, actions, attributes, and scenes, together with a video description and corresponding
counts. The archived records use identity for entity information. Figure C2 presents the profiling template
with this field displayed as entity for consistency with the paper’s terminology. These automatic descriptions
guide candidate selection; annotators construct questions, localize the required evidence, and verify answers.
Deterministic filtering removes 1,454 candidates shorter than 30 seconds, leaving 7,035 videos. A further
89 lack at least one of the four cue types, yielding 6,946 eligible public candidates. The filtering criteria require
positive entity, action, attribute, and scene counts alongside the metadata used by the selection pipeline. These
counts describe the candidate pool before manual task construction, not validated benchmark questions.

C.3 Question-Answering Taxonomy
QA types are grouped by the dominant operation needed to answer each question. Perception covers direct
recognition and judgments from the available evidence, while reasoning covers temporal, causal, or comparative
relations. Both tracks use five answer options, but their type sets differ because AV questions are designed to
draw on speech, sound, or joint audio-visual evidence. The shared format standardizes scoring without equating
difficulty across tracks.
OmniCompass-VL contains eight QA types, listed in Table C3 and shown in Figure C3. Fact Recognition,
Action Recognition, Attribute Perception, Visual Counting, and Spatial Perception form the perception group.

C.4 Reference Design and Provenance
Reference images identify a target through entity, action, attribute, or scene information, as defined in Table C5.
The unit of analysis is a task-specific reference, not a unique image file or video. Each of the 820 VL videos
contributes one reference for TG, SG, and QA, yielding 2,460 task instances. Tasks can select different targets;
their cue types and sources are counted separately.
Entity cues account for 1,960 of the 2,460 references, or 79.7%. The remaining references comprise 171
action cues, 200 attribute cues, and 129 scene cues. The examples include recognizable actions in Sports and
appearance-based vehicle distinctions in Traffic. Figure C4 reports domain-wise frequencies; the cue types are
not equally represented in this set.
The reference set contains 1,933 video-internal images and 527 Internet images. Video-internal crops
preserve the identity of targets specific to a recording, while external exemplars can specify recognizable people,
objects, or actions. Source selection follows the identification needs of each annotation rather than a fixed
per-domain quota. This variation is part of the benchmark’s reference conditions and is not an independently
controlled source-modality experiment.
For video-internal references, annotation guidelines favor a separate view outside the target interval or QA
clue interval whenever one is available. Reviewers also inspect whether the image reveals the answer event,
since temporal separation alone does not rule out semantic leakage. Crops retain target-identifying content
while limiting irrelevant context and direct answer evidence. These checks aim to separate target specification
from the evidence needed to solve the task.

D.1 Review Criteria and Repair Records
Quality control combines semantic review with structural validation. Reviewers check paired-setting target
identity, temporal-event completeness, endpoint box alignment, QA answer uniqueness, and evidence sufficiency.
AV review additionally examines speech or sound dependence and audio-visual correspondence. Returned
corrections undergo task-level re-verification and lead-expert acceptance. Tables D1 and D2 summarize
verification stages and representative issues.

D.2 Human Effort
The construction and verification records yield a retrospective estimate of 1,784 human-hours, or 223 eight-hour
person-days. This comprises 1,280 annotator-hours and 504 lead-expert-hours, as detailed in Table D3. The
estimate covers training, task construction, cross-verification, correction, adjudication, and final auditing. It is
reconstructed from project records rather than continuous time logs and excludes automated processing, model
inference, and waiting periods.

E.1 Inputs and Paired Settings
Frames are sampled uniformly across full videos. The standard budget is 64 for proprietary models and
open-source models above 100B total parameters, and 32 otherwise. GPT-series runs use 49 frames to reserve a
reference slot within the evaluated 50-image input limit; VITA-1.5-7B uses 16. Budgets remain fixed within a
model across tasks and reference settings. Cross-model results therefore reflect the stated input budgets, not a
strictly equal-compute comparison.

Each timestamp is literal text in seconds from the start of the video, placed immediately before its
corresponding frame, following the design of TimeLens (Zhang et al., 2026). The frame numbers index
sampled frames from zero: <frame 0> is the first, and <frame 63> is the last in these 64-frame examples.
Angle-bracketed items denote media content, not literal text tokens. <audio> is the video’s audio track, and
<ref image> is the task-specific reference image. The arrows indicate input order, and ... omits intermediate
timestamp–frame pairs; neither is sent to the model. [Prompt] stands for the task prompt, which includes
the full video duration. The timestamps shown are specific to these clips, not fixed for other videos or frame
budgets. Within each pair, the video evidence, task semantics, and ground truth are shared; only the target
specification changes.
TG returns temporal boundaries, and QA selects one of five answer options. Following V-STaR (Cheng
et al., 2026), SG receives the relevant interval and returns one bounding box at each endpoint. It therefore
evaluates target localization at supplied times, not temporal search or dense tracking throughout the interval.
Figures E1–E6 illustrate response formats with annotated ground truth. The output panels are never supplied to
the models in either setting.
