                                                             Policy-shift distillation reuses the difference between a teacher’s log probabilities
                                                             before and after reinforcement learning (RL) as supervision on student-generated
                                                             trajectories. However, this endpoint policy shift cannot reveal whether local action
                                                             preferences evolved consistently during teacher training. The same endpoint
                                                             shift can result from early and late shifts that reinforce or partially cancel each
                                                             other. We investigate whether agreement between early and late teacher policy
                                                             shifts can guide selective transfer. We introduce Waypoint-guided On-Policy
                                                             Distillation(Waypoint-OPD), which uses an intermediate teacher checkpoint from
                                                             the same RL run to compare early and late shifts in relative action preferences at
                                                             student-visited prefixes. We gate the endpoint shift according to the directional
                                                             agreement between the early and late shifts, suppressing it when they point in
                                                             opposing directions. Compared with endpoint-only transfer, our method requires
                                                             only one additional frozen-teacher scoring pass. Using RL-trained Qwen3-4B
                                                             teachers, we evaluate our method with Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-14B, and Qwen3-30B-
                                                             A3B as student models in mathematical and code reasoning. In all six domain–
                                                             student settings, Waypoint-OPD achieves the highest domain-average score among
                                                             the compared distillation methods. Ablations with the Qwen3-8B student show that
                                                             removing or shuffling gates reduces mathematical reasoning performance. These
                                                             results support the use of directional agreement between early and late teacher
                                                             policy shifts to weight the endpoint shift during student training. Our code is
                                                             available at https://github.com/hhhbxloveai/WaypointOPD.

                                         Reinforcement learning can improve language-model instruction following using rewards learned
                                         from human preferences (Ouyang et al., 2022), and mathematical and code reasoning using verifiable

rewards (Shao et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025). The latter requires repeated generation,
verification, and optimization of on-policy responses. Reusing a teacher’s RL improvements offers a
way to share this training effort across target models. This raises the question: which local changes in
the teacher’s policy should guide a student on its own generated trajectories?
Standard on-policy distillation (OPD) trains the student to match the teacher’s token distributions on
student-generated prefixes (Agarwal et al., 2024). Its supervision remains tied to the teacher’s absolute
policy and capability limitations. On-Policy Delta Distillation (OPD2 ) uses the change between a
reasoning-tuned teacher and its base model, retaining token-level signals when the centered change
agrees in sign with the centered standard distillation signal (Heo et al., 2026). Direct On-Policy
Distillation (Direct-OPD) focuses on RL-induced improvements. It uses the log-probability ratio
between pre-RL and post-RL teacher checkpoints as a dense implicit reward on student-generated
states, transferring the teacher’s policy change without matching its absolute distribution (Feng et al.,
2026). Both methods extract changes from a pair of teacher models, but neither directly measures
whether consecutive changes during teacher RL point in compatible directions.
The Initial-to-Final log-probability ratio captures only the change between the teacher’s policies
before and after RL training. The same endpoint signal can arise from early and late policy changes
that point in similar or opposing directions. Endpoint-only transfer cannot distinguish these cases.
We hypothesize that agreement between the early and late changes provides evidence for deciding
how much weight to assign an endpoint shift at a student-generated prefix.
Building on this hypothesis, we propose Waypoint-OPD, which uses agreement between consecutive
teacher shifts to gate the endpoint transfer signal. We retain three checkpoints from the same teacher
RL run: Initial before RL, Waypoint at an intermediate RL step, and Final at the end of RL. At
each student-generated prefix, we select its top-k most probable next-token candidates. Over these
candidates, we compare how the teacher’s relative preferences change from Initial to Waypoint and
from Waypoint to Final. Their agreement gates the centered Initial-to-Final signal, retaining aligned
changes and suppressing reversals. Waypoint thus supplies evidence for weighting the endpoint shift,
which remains the transferred signal. Relative to endpoint-only transfer, this requires one additional
frozen teacher scoring pass, with no new teacher training or verifier calls during student transfer.
Our contributions are:

       • Temporal agreement as a criterion for selective transfer. We use the Waypoint checkpoint
         to assess temporal agreement at student-generated prefixes, revealing directional information
         unavailable from the endpoint pair alone.
       • Waypoint-guided gating of endpoint signals. We develop Waypoint-OPD, a dense on-
         policy objective that uses this agreement to weight the endpoint signal at each student-
         generated prefix, with one additional frozen teacher scoring pass.
       • Higher average performance across domains and model sizes. We evaluate Qwen3-8B,
         Qwen3-14B, and Qwen3-30B-A3B(Yang et al., 2025) on mathematics and code. Waypoint-
         OPD achieves the highest recorded domain average among the evaluated baselines in all six
         settings (Figure 1 and Tables 1 and 2). Ablations on Qwen3-8B mathematical reasoning
         support the value of temporal gating (Table 3).

2.1   P ROBLEM SETUP AND NOTATION

Let x ∼ D be a prompt, y = (y1 , . . . , yT ) a response, and st = (x, y<t ) the prefix at which a
candidate token a is scored. The student πθ generates on-policy responses y ∼ πθ (· | x). Its
initialization πS remains fixed as a reference during transfer.
We denote the policies of the Initial, Waypoint, and Final teachers by π0 , πm , and π1 , respectively. All
three checkpoints come from the same teacher RL run: Initial before RL, Waypoint at an intermediate
step, and Final after RL. They remain frozen during transfer. We measure the teacher’s RL-induced
change relative to π0 and regularize student updates relative to student initialization πS . Teachers and
student share a tokenizer, allowing direct comparison of probabilities for the same token.

3.2        AGREEMENT GATING AND BATCH - SCALE NORMALIZATION

When both centered segment norms exceed ε = 10−8 , we use the positive-cosine gate
                                                                      gt = max(0, ct ).                                                                (10)
If either norm is at most ε, we use the low-norm fallback gt = 1. The gate gt ∈ [0, 1] is shared by all
candidates at prefix st . Outside this fallback, it removes the endpoint signal when ct ≤ 0 and scales
its magnitude by ct otherwise. Algorithm and implementation conventions are given in Appendix B.
For illustration, consider two decompositions of a nonzero qt -centered endpoint d, both outside the
low-norm fallback. The pair u = v = d/2 gives c = 1 and g = 1, whereas u = 2d, v = −d
gives c = −1 and g = 0. Both yield u + v = d, so endpoint-only transfer cannot distinguish them.
Appendix A.1 derives the endpoint’s independence from Waypoint at fixed student prefixes and
weights. Directional agreement alone does not guarantee beneficial supervision.

4     E XPERIMENTS
We examine three questions:
Q1. Does Waypoint-OPD improve transfer across math, code, and model scales?
Q2. Does temporal consistency improve on endpoint transfer and Final-teacher imitation?
Q3. How do gate assignment, mean normalization, and Waypoint position affect performance?
Supplementary training-time and response-length analyses appear in Appendix H.

4.1   M ODELS , DATA , AND EVALUATION

Teacher checkpoints and student models. For each domain, we use three checkpoints from the
same Qwen3-4B (Yang et al., 2025) RL run: Initial (π0 ) before RL, Waypoint (πm ) at RL step 600,
and Final (π1 ) at RL step 1200. Math and code teachers use non-thinking RL. All three checkpoints
remain frozen during transfer. We train Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-14B, and Qwen3-30B-A3B students
using the teacher’s RL-induced policy shifts. These students span three scales and dense and MoE
architectures within one family with compatible tokenizers. The transfer objective does not require
the teacher to outperform the student. We report all three teacher checkpoints and the Base students
in the result tables to show pre-transfer performance.

Training and evaluation data. Math transfer uses DAPO-Math-17K prompts (Yu et al., 2025).
We evaluate on seven benchmarks: AIME 2024, AIME 2025, AIME 2026, HMMT February 2025,
HMMT November 2025, HMMT February 2026, and AMC 2023. For each problem, mean@32
measures average correctness over 32 generated responses. Benchmark scores average these values
over problems, and Avg. is the unweighted mean across benchmarks.
Code transfer uses a Codeforces-derived prompt set. Evaluation uses the 378-problem MBPP+ set
from EvalPlus (Liu et al., 2023), reporting both the original MBPP tests (Austin et al., 2021) and
the original plus additional tests (MBPP+), together with LiveCodeBench v4, v5, and v6 (Jain et al.,
2025). We report mean@8, which averages correctness over eight generated responses per problem
and then over the problems in each benchmark. Code Avg. is the unweighted mean over these five
evaluation scores. Within each benchmark, methods share prompt templates, decoding parameters,
maximum generation lengths, and sample counts.

Student checkpoint selection. For both domains, we use AIME 2024 as the validation set and
select each trained student’s checkpoint with the highest validation accuracy. All benchmark results
for that student use this selected checkpoint.

Implementation. We build on verl (Sheng et al., 2025) and score the student’s top-16 token
IDs with the three frozen teachers. The transfer objective combines the dense distillation reward
with the student KL penalty. Answer graders are used only for evaluation. Implementation and
training/evaluation configurations appear in Appendices B and C.

4.2   BASELINES AND CONTROLS

We compare against the Base student, standard on-policy distillation (OPD) from the Final teacher
(Agarwal et al., 2024), OPD2 with its sampled-token objective (Heo et al., 2026), and endpoint
Direct-OPD using log π1 − log π0 (Feng et al., 2026). All methods share training and evaluation
settings, except for method-specific hyperparameters.
Three controls on Qwen3-8B math retain centering and alter one component of the full method.
No gate sets gt = 1 while retaining the gate-mean normalization rule. Shuffled gate permutes
gates among valid response prefixes within a batch, retaining each prefix’s candidate weights and
centered endpoint signal. No normalization fixes κB = 1 while retaining the positive-cosine gate.
Appendix E gives definitions and invariants of the shuffle control. We separately vary the math
Waypoint over teacher RL steps 500, 600, and 700 on Qwen3-8B in Appendix F.

5     R ESULTS AND A NALYSIS
We compare transfer performance across math and code at three student scales (Section 5.1). We
examine learning trajectories and performance relative to the Final teacher on Qwen3-8B math
(Section 5.2). We assess the roles of temporal gating, gate assignment, and mean normalization
through ablations (Section 5.3).

5.1   M AIN RESULTS ACROSS DOMAINS AND SCALES

Tables 1 and 2 report math scores and code averages. Full code results appear in Appendix D.
Waypoint-OPD achieves the highest domain-average score among the evaluated student methods in
all six settings.

Math. Waypoint-OPD improves over Direct-OPD, the strongest average baseline for all three
students, by 2.18, 1.59, and 1.89 percentage points for Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-14B, and Qwen3-30B-
A3B, respectively. It improves 19 of 21 benchmark comparisons. The exceptions are HMMT25N
for 14B (47.60 to 47.08) and HMMT26F for 30B-A3B (33.05 to 32.77). These results demonstrate
improvements over endpoint transfer across student scales and both dense and MoE architectures.
The 14B and 30B-A3B students also exceed the Final teacher’s 56.13 average by 3.07 and 1.72 points,
respectively, while 8B essentially matches it at 56.14. The 14B student surpasses the teacher on all
seven math benchmarks.

Code. Waypoint-OPD improves over Direct-OPD by 2.66, 1.52, and 1.19 percentage points on
average for Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-14B, and Qwen3-30B-A3B, respectively, with gains in all 15 code
comparisons. OPD2 achieves higher scores on 14B LCB v5 and on 30B-A3B MBPP and MBPP+,
while Waypoint-OPD retains the highest average for each student (Appendix D). The 14B and 30B-
A3B students also surpass the Final teacher’s 55.37 average by 2.45 and 2.17 points, respectively,
each scoring higher on four of the five code evaluations.

Interpreting the endpoint comparison. The gains over Direct-OPD show that Waypoint-OPD
improves transfer performance across both domains and all three student scales. OPD and OPD2
provide complementary comparisons with Final-teacher imitation and selective distillation based on
advantage sign agreement, respectively.

benchmark. Random reassignment is thus more detrimental than removing the selective weighting
in this setting, supporting the value of matching each gate to the prefix whose temporal agreement
produced it.

Removing mean normalization. Fixing κB = 1 while retaining the positive-cosine gate lowers
Avg. by 0.92 points to 55.22. Normalization improves six of seven benchmarks. The exception
is HMMT February 2025 (34.79 without normalization versus 34.38 with it). The unnormalized
variant still exceeds Direct-OPD by 1.26 points on average and on all seven benchmarks. Thus, the
advantage persists without batch normalization, which provides an additional average benefit here.
The remaining gain cannot be assigned entirely to gating because centering and other differences
from Direct-OPD are also retained.

Ablation insights. These ablations support temporal weighting, its prefix assignment, and an
incremental benefit from normalization in Qwen3-8B math. Their gaps are conditional on the retained
components, not independent additive contributions. For a fixed rollout batch, shuffling preserves the
gate distribution and κB , while advantage and gradient norms depend additionally on the gate–signal
pairing (Appendix E). Supplementary diagnostics show stronger average gate attenuation in later
batches, while mean-gate normalization does not fix the reward norm (Appendix G).

6    R ELATED W ORK

On-policy distillation and policy-shift transfer. Knowledge distillation transfers teacher distri-
butions or generated sequences (Hinton et al., 2015; Kim & Rush, 2016), while on-policy variants
supervise student-generated trajectories (Agarwal et al., 2024). Complementary work refines diver-
gence objectives and the use of teacher- and student-generated data (Wen et al., 2023; Gu et al., 2024;
Ko et al., 2024; 2025). Weak-to-strong generalization asks whether weaker supervision can elicit
stronger capabilities (Burns et al., 2024). WSPO and proxy-tuning reuse a weak model’s adaptation
contrast for training and decoding, respectively (Zhu et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2024). Within on-policy
training, Direct-OPD transfers an RL-induced policy change through an endpoint log-ratio on student
rollouts (Feng et al., 2026). W2S-OPD constructs a proxy teacher by adding a weak-model logit
contrast to student base logits, then minimizes reverse KL on student rollouts. Its contrasts span
RL checkpoints, model scales, and correct versus incorrect hints (Yu et al., 2026). OPRD uses a
centered teacher–reference shift to amplify the component of a verifier-driven gradient along that
direction (Park et al., 2026). Waypoint-OPD builds on Direct-OPD, weighting local endpoint signals
by agreement between shifts before and after a Waypoint checkpoint.

Selective supervision in distillation. Teacher–student compatibility affects the utility of on-policy
supervision (Li et al., 2026). Prune-OPD uses support overlap for suffix attenuation (Yang et al.,
2026). EOPD uses teacher uncertainty in its entropy-dependent forward KL (Jin et al., 2026), while
PW-OPSD uses token position in its reference-conditioned self-distillation (Liu et al., 2026). OPD2
gates sampled-token updates by sign agreement between centered teacher–base and teacher–student
advantages (Heo et al., 2026), whereas RG-OPD gates trajectories using agreement between verifier-
derived advantages and teacher–student sequence log-likelihood gaps (Akhondzadeh et al., 2026).
Waypoint-OPD uses different evidence: consecutive shifts in the teacher’s own RL trajectory over
student-supported candidate actions. Their weighted directional agreement produces a prefix-level
gate for the dense endpoint update, without verifier feedback during transfer. This agreement is a
weighting criterion, not a guarantee of correctness.

Checkpoint selection and progressive distillation. A teacher’s final performance need not deter-
mine its teaching utility: early stopping and intermediate checkpoint selection can improve distillation
(Cho & Hariharan, 2019; Wang et al., 2022). Pro-KD and progressive distillation use successive
teacher checkpoints as targets or a curriculum (Rezagholizadeh et al., 2022; Panigrahi et al., 2025).
Direct-OPD also studies RL checkpoint choice for transfer (Feng et al., 2026). For fixed Initial
and Final endpoints, Waypoint measures directional consistency between the two shifts. It supplies
neither an imitation target nor a curriculum. The centered Initial-to-Final shift remains the transferred
object.

7    C ONCLUSION

We presented Waypoint-OPD, which uses agreement between consecutive teacher shifts to transfer
RL-induced policy changes. An intermediate Waypoint checkpoint reveals whether early and late
changes in relative action preferences reinforce or oppose each other at student-generated prefixes.
This information, unavailable from endpoint differences alone, determines the weight of the centered
Initial-to-Final shift in the student’s on-policy objective. The method requires one additional frozen-
teacher scoring pass and no verifier calls during transfer. Experiments on mathematical and code
reasoning show gains over endpoint transfer across three Qwen3 student scales. Ablations on Qwen3-
8B math support prefix-specific gate assignment and batch-scale normalization. These findings
suggest that intermediate teacher checkpoints provide evidence for weighting local policy changes
during student learning.

Hence Waypoint affects gt and κB , while the transferred endpoint dt remains fixed. This comparison
holds at fixed student prefixes and weights; subsequent training trajectories can differ.
The remaining properties follow directly from the definitions. First, Cqt [z + b] = Cqt [z] for any
action-independent constant b, so such offsets in teacher scores leave the transfer signal unchanged.
Second, outside the low-norm fallback, the gate is one for positively collinear shifts and zero for
nonpositive weighted cosine. Independent positive rescaling of ut and vt leaves this gate unchanged
provided neither segment enters the fallback; their sum dt can change. Finally, gt ∈ [0, 1] and κB > 0
ensure that At (a) cannot reverse the sign of dt (a). The effective multiplier κB gt can exceed one:
batch normalization targets the mean gate, not a fixed reward norm. These are properties of the
construction; the usefulness of temporal weighting is evaluated empirically.

We use the candidate-wise dual-clipped PPO surrogate (Schulman et al., 2017; Ye et al., 2020).
Let πold denote the student policy at rollout time, with rt (a) = πθ (a | st )/πold (a | st ), r̄t (a) =
clip(rt (a), 1 − ϵ, 1 + ϵ), and let c > 1 be the dual-clip constant, distinct from ct . For each candidate,
the maximized surrogate is

                            max{min[rt (a)At (a), r̄t (a)At (a)], cAt (a)}, At (a) < 0.

The loss sums −Jt (a) over candidates and uses token-mean aggregation over valid response tokens,
together with the sampled-token KLPpenalty to πS in Eq. 13. At the rollout policy, rt (a) = 1 and

B.2    I MPLEMENTATION CONVENTIONS

Candidate supports Kt and detached coefficients At (a) remain fixed during each student update.
Batch-scale normalization uses valid response prefixes across the global rollout batch B. The low-
norm fallback retains the endpoint signal when a segment has too little weighted variation to define
a reliable direction, using ε = 10−8 . The normalization floor εnum is the smallest positive normal
float32 value (≈ 1.18 × 10−38 ), distinct from the low-norm threshold. Training and evaluation
configurations appear in Appendix C.
